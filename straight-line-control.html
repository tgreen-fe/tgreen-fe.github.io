Straight-line control on a differential-drive robot

A warehouse robot that cannot hold a straight line hits things. We compared four ways of making a small differential-drive robot do it, ending with a network trained on the robot itself to imitate the best of the others. Fusing two heading sources is what actually solved the problem. The learned controller did not improve on it, and the data shows why.

The problem

Give two nominally identical motors the same PWM and the robot curves. Ours curved left, about 0.6 m off the line over a two-metre run, and it was not broken — that is simply what manufacturing tolerance, tyre wear and an imperfect floor add up to. In a warehouse the same effect is what puts a robot into a rack.

The interesting question is not whether you can correct it, but what you have to know about the robot to correct it. A gain you tuned on the bench does not survive a payload that sits over one wheel. So the brief we set ourselves was to work up from the crudest controller to one that learns its own correction, and measure the same thing at every step: how far off the line the robot ends up.

Four controllers

  • Open-loop PWM. Same duty cycle to both motors. The baseline that shows the size of the problem.
  • PID per wheel. Closed loop on wheel speed from the encoders, gains tuned by Ziegler-Nichols. This corrects the motors against each other, which is not the same as driving straight — it halved the drift and no more.
  • Fused-heading correction. Steering on heading rather than wheel speed, where heading comes from odometry corrected by the gyro. The gyro is noisy but sees real rotation; odometry is smooth but blind to slip. Neither is usable alone.
  • A network trained on the robot. Five inputs, one hidden layer of six units, 43 parameters, running in a 30 ms control step on the ATmega32U4 alongside everything else. It is first trained by copying the fused-heading controller's steering commands, then tuned online against a shaped score. Trained weights are written to EEPROM so a policy survives a power cycle.

Measuring it honestly

The robot reports its own position from odometry, which is the thing under test, so the primary measurement had to come from outside it. Graph paper ran the length of the track, a 3D-printed jig set the robot on the same starting pose every time, and the finishing offset was read off the paper with a tape. Odometry was logged as well, and keeping both turned out to matter more than expected.

What happened

The escalation works, up to a point. PID roughly halved the open-loop drift. Fusing the two heading sources removed almost all of it: unweighted, the robot finished within a centimetre or two of the line, run after run.

The learned controller did not beat that. Unweighted it finished about 0.03 m off, against 0.01 m, and its runs were far less repeatable. It earned its keep only under load: with 150 g over one wheel it averaged around 0.05 m off the line where the fused-heading controller averaged 0.11 m.

Why it lost

The answer was already in the logs. Comparing what the robot's odometry reported at the end of a run against the tape measurement, the two disagree — by up to a quarter of a metre. That gap is the robot's own position error, and it is the signal the controller was steering on.

The fusion weights odometry heavily — the gyro contributes a small correction term, not an equal vote — so the fused heading inherits odometry's slow drift. The network was then trained to copy a PI controller running on that same estimate. It learned, correctly, that odometry predicts the expert's output; it also learned to ignore the disagreement between the two sensors, which was the one input that could have told it the estimate was going wrong.

Which makes this less a failure of the network than of what it was given to copy. Two changes would be worth trying: weight the gyro more heavily in the fusion before generating any training data at all, and train over a distance long enough for drift to show up in the score. The runs here were about three metres, and that is roughly where the divergence starts.

What I take from it

The learned controller was the interesting part of the brief and the least useful part of the result. What made straight-line driving work was noticing that two mediocre heading sources fail in different ways and combining them — and what made the result legible was measuring from outside the robot rather than trusting the number it prints.

The result went against the hypothesis, which is the kind of thing that is only useful if you can point at the cause. Here the cause is in the odometry comparison above rather than in an argument about it, and that is the part of the work I would stand behind.

This was a three-person study for the Robotics Systems unit, with Callum Edwards and Leon Weyrich. I wrote the embedded control stack — drive loop, odometry-gyro fusion, the PI expert, calibration and logging — designed and built the test setup, and ran the experiments.
