Adding touch to a pretrained vision-language-action model

Pretrained VLAs are trained on vision and language. There's no settled way to give one a tactile signal, so I'm adding one to a pretrained model and testing whether what the robot feels changes what it does.

Context
MSc Robotics, University of Bristol
Role
Sole author, supervised
Period
June to September 2026
Status
In progress, due September

The question

A vision-language-action model maps what the robot sees and what it has been told into what it should do next. The pretrained ones are trained on vision and language, because that's what there is a lot of. Touch is different: there's far less of it, it's sensor-specific, and it doesn't arrive in a format the backbone was built to read.

So there's a choice about how to present it, and it's usually settled by convention rather than tested. You can render the tactile reading as an image and give it one of the model's camera inputs, which is convenient because optical-tactile sensors produce images anyway. Or you can process it first and hand the backbone something smaller, in this case the material probabilities from a separate classifier, appended to the robot's state vector.

Each has an argument for it, and the comparison is about which one a pretrained model can actually use.

The task, and why it changed

The project started on cloth folding. Two fabrics can look near-identical and behave completely differently under the gripper, so folding by material seemed like the natural place for touch to matter. The early fine-tunes on the YuMi could produce the fold motion, but they couldn't show whether any input beyond vision was steering the behaviour, and that question is the whole dissertation.

So the task changed to one built to make that question answerable. The materials the robot touches now look identical to the camera and differ only in texture, and the instruction never says which target is correct. The robot touches, then has to act on what it felt: each material sends the arm to a different target. Vision can't solve this even in principle, so if the policy is right more often than chance, the information came through the tactile channel.

How it is set up

  • A GelTip-style optical tactile sensor, built for the project: a silicone gel membrane over an internal camera and lights. The first build leaked ambient light through the silicone, so the working version sits in a sealed opaque housing.
  • A gate before any integration: a standalone classifier has to tell the materials apart from imprints alone, evaluated across recording sessions instead of random frame splits, so it can't pass by memorising one session's conditions.
  • SmolVLA as the backbone, LoRA fine-tuned on a single consumer GPU. I ran OpenVLA and π0.5 on the same data early on; SmolVLA was the most repeatable on this robot, so it carried the rest of the project.
  • More than 300 demonstration episodes on the YuMi, collected with a click-to-target tool that scripts the motion and logs every episode's start, target and layout alongside the frames.
  • Controls built into the experiment rather than added afterwards: rollouts run in pairs from a fixed start, the materials swap between targets so the behaviour has to follow the touch, and an ablation replaces the imprint with a blank reference frame, which should erase the effect.

Where it stands

The pieces work. The sensor is built and sealed, the classifier gate passed, and language conditioning has been demonstrated on the robot: the policy reaches for the target the prompt names, and physically swapping the targets swaps the reach. The tactile channel is trained and has been shown steering the policy live on the YuMi, with the imprint as the only input that could carry the information. The blank-reference ablation erases the effect, which is the check that the model is reading the imprint and not something else in the pipeline.

What's left is scale and the final evaluation. I'm collecting a larger dataset now and the last gates run over the coming weeks. The dissertation is due in September, and I'll put the numbers here once it's submitted.
